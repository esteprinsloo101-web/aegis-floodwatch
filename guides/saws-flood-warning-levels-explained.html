SAWS flood warnings explained: yellow, orange, red and levels 1–10

When heavy rain is on the way, the South African Weather Service (SAWS) issues impact-based warnings with a colour and a level, for example "Orange Level 6: disruptive rain". This guide explains what those labels mean, what to do at each one, and how planners add a simple flood screen on top. Official SAWS and disaster-management warnings always come first.

How the warning level is chosen

SAWS combines two things: the impact the weather could have (minor, significant or severe) and the likelihood of that impact (very low to high). The combination gives a level from 1 to 10 and a colour.

Likelihood ↓ / Impact →MinorSignificantSevere
HighYellow 2Orange 6Red 10
MediumYellow 1Orange 5Red 9
Low—Yellow 4Orange 8
Very low—Yellow 3Orange 7

Levels 1–4 are yellow, 5–8 orange and 9–10 red. A higher number doesn't only mean more rain. It can also mean the same rain falling where it is more likely to cause harm, for example after days of wet weather or near informal settlements on low ground.

What to do at each colour

ColourTypical flood impactsWhat to do
YellowLocalised flooding of roads, low-lying bridges and settlements, and some traffic disruptionCheck the warning area and plan routes that avoid low-water crossings. Charge phones.
OrangeFlooded roads and settlements, danger to life from fast-flowing streams, and disruption to servicesAvoid travel where you can. Move vehicles and valuables off low ground. Know your route to higher ground.
RedWidespread flooding, washed-away roads, and possible loss of life and displacementFollow instructions from local disaster management, including evacuation. Go to higher ground early.

At any level: never walk or drive through flood water. Moving water that looks shallow can sweep people and cars away, and the road underneath may be gone. Schools and parents should plan for children who cross streams to get to school.

Where to get the official warning

  • SAWS: weathersa.co.za for current warnings and the impact tables behind them.
  • Your municipality's disaster management centre for local instructions and evacuation points.

For planners: a simple river flood screen

Emergency planners and engineers sometimes run a quick screening check on a river reach, while always staying under the official warnings. A common starting point is Manning's equation:

Q = (1/n) · A · R^(2/3) · S^(1/2)

Worked example (illustrative numbers): roughness n = 0.035, flow area A = 40 m², hydraulic radius R = 1.8 m, slope S = 0.001. Then Q ≈ (1/0.035) × 40 × 1.8^(2/3) × 0.001^(1/2) ≈ 53 m³/s. That is the discharge this cross-section carries at that depth. If the forecast discharge for the reach is well above it, water is likely to leave the channel. That calls for a closer look, not a conclusion.

A screen like this ignores bridges, debris, backwater and many other real effects. It helps you decide where to look first. It does not forecast a flood.

FAQ

What does an Orange Level 6 warning mean?

It means SAWS expects significant impacts, such as flooded roads and settlements and danger to life from fast-flowing water, with a high likelihood. Avoid travel where you can and follow local disaster management.

What is the difference between yellow, orange and red warnings?

Levels 1–4 are yellow (minor to significant impacts, lower likelihood), 5–8 are orange (significant to severe impacts) and 9–10 are red (severe impacts with medium to high likelihood).

Is AEGIS Floodwatch an official warning service?

No. AEGIS is a screening product built on public science feeds. SAWS and your local disaster management centre are the official sources.

Is the AEGIS map free?

Yes. The live map and the Get me to safety panel are free. The R89 Science Desk adds screening methods, a lead-time table and a planner workflow.

Can floods be predicted exactly?

No. River floods can often be seen days ahead in models. Flash floods and dam breaks can develop within minutes to hours, so follow official warnings.
